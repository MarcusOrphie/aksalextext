How to Not Quit Your Blog in Week Two and Avoid Burnout

Alex Aksenov · September 8, 2026 · 5 min read

What's really happening in week two

The first days run on the dopamine of something new: a fresh idea, the reaction of your first readers, the feeling of a launch. That resource is finite and runs out around day seven to ten. If posting hasn't become routine by then, every next post takes willpower rather than habit.

Add to that the fatigue of everyday life on maternity leave, where there isn't much energy to spare in the first place - and it becomes clear why blogs most often go quiet right here.

The starter mistake: a pace that's too high

On enthusiasm it's easy to promise yourself a post every day, or even several formats at once. The problem is that a pace built for peak motivation can't survive an ordinary day with no sleep and a sick child.

Better a blog that's been going out once every three days for six months than one that went out daily and shut down in two weeks.

It's worth starting at a pace you can keep up in a bad week, not your best one.

How to build a pace that doesn't break

SituationWhat to do
A good day, plenty of energyWrite the full post and prep a topic for next time
An average dayPublish a short note instead of a full post
A hard daySkip the post without guilt - it's not the end of the blog
No energy at allSave the thought in your notes and come back to it later

A scale like this removes the all-or-nothing pressure that turns a single bad day into an abandoned blog.

Signs you're heading toward burnout

What to watch for

  • Posting brings irritation rather than relief
  • You compare yourself to bloggers who publish every day
  • You've started to feel the blog is a duty, not a choice
  • The joy of your followers' responses is gone

If you recognize yourself in even two of these, it's time to ease the pace, not force yourself to write more.

What makes a blog sustainable in the long run

Sustainability rests not on discipline but on posting being built into a daily routine that already exists - for example, always during the same nap or the same walk. Then the decision "to write or not" isn't made from scratch every time.

The second thing is having topics and drafts ready in advance. When you don't have to invent anything from nothing, posting turns into a 15-20 minute task rather than a project.

If the blog is already almost abandoned

A pause in a blog isn't a failure, it's a normal part of the journey. You can come back after a week or a month of silence with no explanations and no apologies: the followers who care will stay, and a formal report on why you paused is something no one needs.

Start with one short post, without trying to make up for everything at once. Your pace comes back gradually, the same way it was lost.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are blogs most often abandoned specifically in the second week?

The first week runs on the enthusiasm of a new venture, and that runs out around day seven to ten. If posting hasn't become a habit by then, every post takes willpower, and motivation quickly falls short.

What posting pace should I choose at the start?

The one you can keep up on your worst day, not your best. It's better to post steadily once every three days than daily until the first stumble.

How do I know I'm heading toward blog burnout?

The signs: posting brings irritation instead of relief, the joy of responses is gone, the blog feels like a duty. If that's you, ease the pace rather than forcing yourself to write more.

Can I take a break from the blog without losing followers?

Yes. The followers who genuinely care about your blog stay even after a break. You can come back with no apologies - one short post is enough.

What helps you keep blogging steadily on maternity leave?

Tying posting to a routine that already exists and having topics prepared in advance. This removes extra decisions and turns posting into a 15-20 minute task.
