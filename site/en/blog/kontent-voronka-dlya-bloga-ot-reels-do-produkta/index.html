A Content Funnel for Your Blog: From Reels to Product

Alex Aksenov · September 6, 2026 · 5 min read

What a Content Funnel Is and Why a Blog Needs One

A content funnel is a sequence of formats that leads a viewer from a random scroll to a deliberate action: following, clicking into the blog, buying. At each stage the viewer gets their portion of value and gradually gets to know you better.

Without a funnel, a blog works as one-off bursts of attention: a video takes off, racks up views, and then the viewer forgets about the blog until the next random impression. With a funnel, every format works toward the next step instead of existing on its own.

What a Funnel from Reels to Product Consists Of

The Main Stages

  • Reach - short videos seen by new people who don't know you yet
  • Engagement - content that holds attention and sparks interest in the author
  • Trust - more personal or expert content where the viewer gets to know you better
  • Warm-up - material that shows the product's value in practice
  • Product - a concrete offer with a clear next step

You don't have to go through all the stages in separate videos - sometimes one format handles several tasks. But it's important to understand which stage a specific viewer is at, so you don't sell to someone seeing you for the first time.

What the Funnel Looks Like in Practice

Below is an example of how different formats can cover different stages of the funnel.

StageContent formatGoal for the viewer
ReachA short video with a strong hookNotice it and watch to the end
EngagementA series of videos on one topicStart recognizing the author and their style
TrustA personal story, a breakdown of mistakesFeel that the author can be trusted
Warm-upA case study or a breakdown of a resultSee the product's value through an example
ProductA direct offer with a call to actionTake a clear next step
A funnel works not because there's a call to buy at the end, but because every previous step made that call the logical next move.

How to Keep the Viewer at Each Stage

The most common funnel failure is a gap between stages. The viewer watched an entertaining video, and the next touchpoint is a hard sell - it comes across as jarring and breeds distrust. Transitions between stages should be gradual.

It helps to guide the viewer not only through videos but through other touchpoints too: a newsletter subscription, saved posts, pinned stories. That way a person doesn't get lost between stages, even if they don't see every video in a row.

Common Mistakes When Building a Funnel

Many try to sell right from the first touch - that rarely works, because trust hasn't been built yet. The other extreme is endless warm-up with no concrete offer, which leaves the viewer never understanding what you're actually selling.

  • Selling in the first video a new viewer sees
  • No clear next step in the content at the warm-up stage
  • Ignoring the engagement stage - jumping straight from reach to product
  • The same tone of delivery across every stage of the funnel

Frequently Asked Questions

Where do I start building a content funnel if I don't have one yet?

First look at which formats you already have in the blog and sort them into stages - reach, engagement, trust, product. It often turns out there's a shortage of material for the middle of the funnel.

How many videos do I need at each stage of the funnel?

There's no fixed number - go by whether the viewer has time to get to know you and the product enough that the next step looks logical rather than abrupt.

Can I sell right away in short reach videos?

You can, but carefully - that format is better at showing value through an example than through a direct call, otherwise a new viewer scrolls past it.

How do I know the viewer is ready to see the product?

Usually it's the people who have already interacted with the blog before - watched several videos, saved posts, or subscribed to the newsletter.

Do I need to rebuild the funnel if the product changes?

Yes, it's worth revisiting the warm-up and product stages - reach and engagement usually stay workable, while the warm-up needs to be adapted to the new value.
