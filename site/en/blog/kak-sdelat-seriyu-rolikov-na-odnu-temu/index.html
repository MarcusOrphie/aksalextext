How to Make a Series of Videos on One Topic

Sasha Aksenov · September 5, 2026 · 5 min read

A single video lives for one day in the feed, while a series works for weeks - the viewer comes back for the next part. But putting together a series is trickier than it looks: the topic has to genuinely support several parts, not be artificially stretched. Let's figure out how to pick that kind of topic and structure the parts so each one holds attention on its own while leading into the next.

Why make a series instead of one long video

A series solves two problems at once. First, it gives the viewer a reason to subscribe and come back - they want to know what happens next. Second, it takes the pressure off the creator to cram everything into one video: a topic can be explored more deeply if it's spread across several episodes.

Platform algorithms also favor series - a viewer who finishes the first part is more likely to watch the second one too, and that's a signal that the content holds attention.

How to choose a topic that can carry a series

Not every topic works for multiple parts. If an idea fits into one minute without losing meaning, splitting it up artificially is a bad idea - viewers can feel it and they leave.

Signs a topic can support a series

  • The topic has natural stages or steps
  • The story has development, not just a single fact
  • You can show a process from start to result
  • Each part answers its own separate question for the viewer

Series structure: what to build the parts from

A working series is usually built on a clear pattern, where each part performs its own function.

Series partJobWhat to leave at the end
FirstHook with the topic and outline the scale of the problemA question you'll answer later
Middle partsReveal details step by stepA small hook to the next part
FinalDeliver the outcome and resultA call to watch the whole series if some parts were missed

How to keep viewers engaged between parts

A series falls apart if there's too big a gap between parts, or if each part can be watched separately from the rest without losing meaning. Keep the release rhythm predictable, and make it clear in every part that it's part of a series, not a standalone video.

  • Publish parts at a consistent interval, not randomly
  • Briefly remind viewers at the start of each part what the previous one was about
  • Pin a link or playlist with the whole series in your profile description
  • End each part with a specific hook, not a generic "to be continued"
People finish watching a series not because it's long, but because every part answers its own question and opens the next one.

Common mistakes when launching a series

The first mistake is stretching the series longer than the material allows, just to rack up more parts. Viewers feel the artificial padding and lose interest before the finale.

The second mistake is changing the format or tone between parts. If the first part was a personal story and the third suddenly turns into a dry list of tips, the series stops feeling like a cohesive whole.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many parts should a video series have?

There's no universal number - go by how much material the topic has. Usually three to five parts are enough to cover a topic without stretching it artificially.

How often should I publish parts of a series?

It's best to keep a consistent interval, for example every day or every other day - this way the viewer gets used to the rhythm and doesn't forget about the series.

Can parts of a series be watched separately, without the rest?

Each part should make sense on its own, but with a clear indication that it's part of a series - that nudges viewers to watch the rest.

What should I do if the series isn't getting views starting from the second part?

Check whether the connection to the first part got lost - the viewer may not have understood that this is a continuation. Add a clearer reminder at the start.

Does the series format work for complex technical topics?

Yes, a series works great for topics with stages and processes - each part covers one step, and the viewer isn't overloaded with information all at once.
